How the combination operators work

Centroid Balanced Geometric mean Textual Side by side

Every operator answers the same question: which word sits between word A and word B? Each candidate word w gets two numbers: how similar it is to A (sa = cos(w, a)) and how similar it is to B (sb = cos(w, b)). The operators differ only in how they turn those two numbers into one score.

1. Centroid: the smallest angle to the midpoint

The real vectors have hundreds of dimensions, but any two arrows from the same point always lie in a single flat plane. The floor of this picture is that plane, holding a, b and their midpoint ĉ at their real angles. Every other word has part of its vector in the plane and part sticking out of it. That hidden part becomes height here. Every vector has length 1, so every word lands on the surface of a dome of radius 1. Words high up on the dome are mostly unrelated to both inputs.

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom, and hover over a dot to see its word. The dome is shaded by score: darker means higher. The top words are drawn as colored arrows, with a dashed line showing each one's hidden part.

Centroid adds the two vectors and rescales the sum to length 1 to get ĉ. The word with the smallest angle to ĉ wins. The arcs near the origin mark each top word's angle to ĉ. The score is the cosine of that angle.

Because every vector has length 1, the dot product splits into two parts:

cos(w, ĉ) = w·(a + b) / ‖a + b‖ = (sa + sb) / ‖a + b‖

‖a + b‖ depends only on A and B, so it is the same number for every candidate:

So centroid ranks words by sa + sb: losing 0.1 of similarity to one input is made up by gaining 0.1 to the other. That's why a word that leans heavily toward one input, like a near-synonym, can still win.

2. Balanced: inside both circles first

Balanced only gives a word a score of L if it is at least that similar to a and to b. On the same dome, "at least that similar to a" means "inside a circle around a". Drag the Circles slider to grow a circle around a and one around b at the same rate. Only the words inside both circles are highlighted. The first word inside both circles wins, and the slider starts at exactly that size.

A dot close to only one input never gets inside the other circle, however close it is to that one input.

The arcs show each highlighted word's angle to a and to b. The larger angle (thick arc) is the word's weaker side, and it sets the score. The smaller one (thin arc) only has to stay below it, and how far below doesn't matter.

3. Geometric mean: the biggest rectangle

Geometric mean multiplies the two similarities and takes the square root: √(sa · sb). Picture a rectangle that is sa wide and sb tall. The geometric mean is the side of the square with the same area.

A long, thin rectangle (close to one input, far from the other) has a small area even with one long side. So a word can lean toward one input, but losing half its similarity on one side takes doubling the other side to make up for it. That's in between centroid, where any loss can be made up by an equal gain, and balanced, where nothing makes up for the weaker side.

What about textual?

The fourth operator in operators.py skips the vector arithmetic. It embeds a phrase like "earring and modem" and finds the word closest to that phrase's vector. The transformer reads both words together, so it can capture a combined meaning that a plain average can't. That needs the model at run time to embed the new phrase, which the Python backend does on demand: its picks are the textual column in the table above.
